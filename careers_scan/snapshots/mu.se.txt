What are the Principles of Leadership?
Results every day
Every day ends with something done and visible to others. Progress is not a result. Each day is a small life: plan, deliver, show.
Example: end the day with a short note to your team — what is done.
"I need dirt"
Ship imperfect work early, under your own name and at your own risk. If you are not scared of how a release will land, you shipped too late. You over-waited, over-prepared, and paid for it with a lost opportunity. That trade does not work for us.
Every iteration has flaws. That's normal.
Iterate. "If I feel comfortable with a first release, I did it too late."
Dirt is for first versions. What must last — see "Create things that will last for years".
Radical Ownership
You own the result personally. Say "I", not "we". Don't blame circumstances or other people.
One owner, a plan, a date. Every project has one DRI — the one who owns the decision and the result. Every meaningful discussion ends with an owner and a date. Numbers and links, not general answers.
My deadlines can be trusted. Name a date and deliver by it. If you will miss it, say so before it passes, with a new date. Don't say "I was doing", say "I did".
No excuses, only solutions. Anticipate problems and prepare alternatives: if you need a bank account in three days, apply to five banks at once.
Act by default. If there is no one to consult, use common sense and decide yourself.
If the team fails, the leader owns it first.
Disagree with numbers, then commit. Challenge how we execute, not what we decided, and bring data, not opinions. Without numbers it is a battle of arguments; changing a decision takes real evidence. Once decided, you commit fully.
Deep dive
Know the details of your area and audit it often. When the metrics and what actually happens don't match, trust what happens and dig.
Content is more important than form.
Surface problems early, especially your own.
Example: the dashboard says retention is up, but support tickets grow — go read the tickets.
Hands-on
We don't want managers in the classic sense: people who come to a meeting someone else prepared, without their own document, and leave without written outcomes.
Come with your own document. Leave with written decisions, owners and dates. A meeting without a decision is a waste.
"Someone on my team didn’t prepare the data" is not an excuse. If you see it won’t be ready in time, do it yourself or solve it another way — but yourself.
Example: the report is due tomorrow and the analyst is out — you pull the numbers yourself, or you reframe the question so the data you already have answers it.
Create things that will last for years
Fix problems so they stay fixed. Don’t redirect them or pass them down the line.
A process, ritual or standard you build keeps running when you are not in the room and when people change.
If it stops when its owner leaves, it was not built — it was carried.
Example: every month you fix a broken payment report by hand — that is carrying. You fix the source so the report is right on its own — that is building.
"Go to Eleven"*
Set the bar higher than feels reasonable, and keep going:
When everyone says "this is the maximum", that is where we start. Eleven is about ambition — the bar we set and the goals we take on, not about polishing. Aim at eleven, ship dirt early.
Keep business margins healthy in every decision.
Knowledge goes out of date. Don't be dogmatic. Be open.
Find solutions that create time instead of consuming it. Bureaucracy consumes time; automation creates it.
Sharpen the ax. You won't get unusually good results without changing your approach and trying new things.
To learn teamwork, first learn to work alone. A strong team is a collective of ones, not zeros.
"Embrace Venus"
Ask for help when you need it and offer it to others. Venus de Milo was the emblem in our logo; she stands for love, mutual support and assistance. Don't create problems for others — live your life and let others live theirs without being a shithead.
What's your interview process?
Initial contact will be made via email by one of our recruiters to better understand your relevant experience, ambitions, and interests that could make you a good fit for Muse.
The next step is an interview with HR. A recruiter will ask about your relevant skills and experience, as well as what you're looking for in a new opportunity. During the conversation you'll learn more about the role and our culture. Feel free to ask our recruiters any questions you have at this point.
If all goes well you’ll proceed to the next step, an interview with the hiring manager.
At this stage, hiring managers or tech leads will shortlist candidates and further examine your experience by setting a task for you to complete. You will also be given an overview of the team and what is expected in the role.
Usually, the whole process involves 2-3 interviews.
Which locations are you hiring in?
As a remote-first company, we are open to hiring worldwide. Our HQ is in Limassol, Cyprus. We also have offices in Bury St. Edmunds, UK, Heerenveen, Netherlands, and Milwaukee, US. As well as a large distribution and production facility in Winona, US and in Milan, Italy. For a few roles, it is essential that candidates are based in one of these locations. When necessary, we will include the location requirement in the job description.
What benefits do you offer?
Your benefits, including maternity leave and pension contributions, will depend on your country of residence. More information will be provided by our team during the interview process.
What does the onboarding process look like?
When a candidate accepts our offer, we send them a link to our pre-boarding landing page where the entire onboarding process is described in as much detail as possible.
We follow an asynchronous way of working, therefore our onboarding process is built to accommodate the whole team, wherever they are in the world. In most cases, onboarding is done through video calls, online documents, and courses.
Every newcomer is assigned a People Partner, a "buddy" who will walk you through onboarding plans. These plans include ways to learn our tools, documentation, and intro meetings with your colleagues.
How long will my probation period be?
Your probation period depends on the laws in your country of employment. In many cases, probation lasts for 3 months, with the exception of C-level positions—as it takes more time to deliver results in these roles.
How do you evaluate candidates during the interview process?
We focus on evaluating every candidate's experience, values, thought processes, skills, and alignment with tasks, rather than subjective traits. We also consider factors that contribute to a candidate's long-term compatibility with our culture and projects.
How quickly are you looking to fill a position?
We value a thorough and careful hiring process that focuses on quality, rather than speed. While we are committed to filling positions in a timely manner, our priority is ensuring that each step of our hiring process receives the attention and care it deserves to find the best fit for the role.
Can I reapply if I'm not selected for a position?
Yes of course, you are welcome to reapply for future positions that interest you. Maybe another role that suits you better will come up.
I applied for a position, what are the next steps?
After you submit your application through our careers website, you will receive a confirmation email from us. We carefully review all applications and respond to every candidate.
If your experience matches the requirements of a specific role, one of our team members will reach out to you.
If, for any reason, your experience turns out not to be a match, we will still get back to you with feedback via email. Please know that this decision is not a reflection of your skills or potential.
We always value your time and effort. Good luck!
